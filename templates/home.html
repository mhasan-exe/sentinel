The danger zone: "the open internet"

Between your browser and our server, your traffic passes through equipment you don't control — your Wi-Fi router, your ISP, backbone routers. Anyone with a foothold there can read raw traffic unless it's encrypted first. That's what Hacker View puts on screen: the attacker's exact vantage point at that midpoint.

What TLS does about it

TLS wraps your connection in a private tunnel before any chat data leaves your browser. It does three things: encrypts the data (no one in the middle can read it), authenticates the server (you know you're really talking to Sentinel), and protects integrity (no one can silently edit the bytes in transit). Turn "TLS" off on the dashboard and all three disappear at once.

Where each layer actually sits
  • TLS / ML-KEM-768 — negotiates a shared secret key before any message is sent, so no plaintext ever hits the wire.
  • Ed25519 / ML-DSA-65 — signs the handshake so the server's identity can't be spoofed mid-connection.
  • JWT authentication — proves who's sending a message once the tunnel exists.
  • Input validation — stops malicious payloads (like the XSS demo) from executing even if they slip through.
  • Message integrity — detects if a byte was tampered with in transit.
Why "post-quantum" (PQC) at all?

Today's TLS relies on X25519/Ed25519 — math that's secure against classical computers, but that a sufficiently powerful quantum computer could break. An attacker can record encrypted traffic today and decrypt it years later once that hardware exists ("harvest now, decrypt later"). ML-KEM-768 and ML-DSA-65 are NIST-standardized replacements built to resist that. This lab lets you toggle between the classical pair and the PQC pair on the same live connection and measure the real handshake-time difference — not an estimate, an actual timed run.
